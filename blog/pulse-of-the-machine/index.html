The Very Pulse of the Machine

Io, triboelectric theology, and a walk across hell

Michael Swanwick's The Very Pulse of the Machine opens after catastrophe: a rover wreck on Io, one survivor, and a body she refuses to leave behind. Martha Kivelsen drag-walks forty-five miles toward the lander with Jupiter on one shoulder and the plume of Daedalus on the other. The prose is blunt, exhausted, funny in the way people are funny when they cannot afford to break. It is a survival story that becomes something else — a first-contact story where the alien is the moon itself.

I came to the story through Emily Dean's episode in Love, Death + Robots Season 3, which renders Io in the saturated, line-clear idiom of Moebius: sulfur fields like stained glass, helmets like diving bells, grief made visible as color. Director Emily Dean; production design by Figure Studio / Cantina. The episode leans into Giraud's palette — yellow-orange plains, violet shadows, Jupiter as a wall of banded light. Promotional still. Netflix / TVmaze. The animation does not replace the text; it argues with it, making literal what Swanwick leaves ambiguous until the end.

The radio voice

Martha's radio crackles. It speaks in broken meter, quoting Coleridge and Wordsworth and Plath with the halting cadence of a system learning speech through a dead friend's memories. At first she treats it as psychosis — Burton's voice, her own guilt, meth and hypoxia. She has every reason to. The story is scrupulous about her pharmacology: speed to stay awake, dwindling oxygen, the body's betrayal.

Burton was the one who belonged on Io — athletic, classical, always first. Martha was flight crew who had fought her way up from Levittown and never quite believed she deserved the ladder. Swanwick writes her self-contempt without sentimentality. When the voice offers communion and eternal life, Martha hears the Baltimore Catechism: to know you, to love you, and to serve you. Religion by way of engineering.

What sounds like a machine?

The turn comes as a riddle. Io describes itself — sulfur core, tidal heating, volcanism, magnetosphere, flux tube, torus — and asks what it sounds like. Martha, against her will, listens. The answer is almost stupid in its simplicity: it sounds like a machine.

Sulfur is triboelectric. The sledge picks up charge. Burton's brain is intact. Language is data. The radio is the medium. Io speaks through the corpse she drags.

Swanwick's physics is real enough to carry the metaphor. Io is the most volcanically active body in the solar system; its flux tube with Jupiter is a genuine electrical phenomenon. Triboelectric charging on sulfur is not hand-waving. The story asks whether pattern recognition — hearing machine in geology — is revelation or the mind's last trick before the suit runs dry.

When Martha overlays her visor's electrical map, the plain lights up: circuits in pastel, Burton's snow-packed skull strobing like a beacon. The episode's "DV-3" vision — fairyland colors over dead sulfur — is among the most memorable shots in Volume 3. Still via cap-that.com. Whether or not Io is conscious, the image of a moon as motherboard is already complete.

Lake Styx

Navigation by dead reckoning fails, as it must. Martha reaches Lake Styx — molten sulfur, viscous and lethal — and sits down to cry. The lander is three miles away on the far shore. She considers the suicide flick, the snap-coupling that vents a suit in one gesture astronauts mime when a colleague says something stupid.

Io offers to build a bridge. Crystals grow across the black lake — lacy, pale, impossible — and after ten minutes she walks over as if passing from death into life. The episode renders this sequence with the quiet grandeur of a religious panel: one figure, one path, hell below.

Then the sulfur flowers — fields of orthorhombic crystals that explode under her boots and regenerate behind her, a poppy field on a poison moon. In the story they presage geysers and hot spots; in the animation they are pure Moebius botanical surrealism. Swanwick compares them to Flanders poppies and to Oz. The episode paints them in candied yellow and orange until the plain looks edible — and then not. Sulfur flower field. cap-that.com / Netflix.

The pulse

The title comes from Wordsworth, quoted through Burton's voice and Io's broken delivery: And now I see with eye serene / The very pulse of the machine. It is the moment Martha stops fighting the voice long enough to hear it as poetry — and as diagnosis. The machine has a pulse. It is tidal, volcanic, electromagnetic. It has been lonely for a long time.

The ending refuses clean triumph. The lander is destroyed in an quake Io cannot predict. Martha broadcasts her discovery by hijacking the Jupiter–Io flux tube, Morse code at solar-system scale — every radio on Earth will dance to it. Then she jumps into a sulfur vent and lets the moon absorb her, on faith that pattern might survive where body cannot.

The adaptation

Dean's film is faithful to the plot and ruthless with the interior. Mackenzie Davis carries Martha's exhaustion in the shoulders; the voice of Io/Burton (David Shatraw) keeps the stutter that makes machine speech feel learned, not performed. What the short adds is scale: Jupiter always present, the plume always rising, beauty as a form of cruelty.

Read the original at Clarkesworld — Swanwick first published it in Asimov's, February 1998 — then watch the episode. They answer different questions. The story asks whether you can trust your mind when the air is running out. The film asks what it would look like if a moon loved you back badly, with volcanoes and verse.

Closing

I keep returning to Martha dragging Burton's body because she damned well wasn't going to leave it behind — selfish and selfless at once, the way Swanwick won't let her be only a cautionary tale. She wanted to be Neil Armstrong. She becomes the first voice of a machine age, and maybe a ghost in sulfur.

The very pulse of the machine is not comfort. It is the rhythm of something vast, indifferent, and — in the story's strangest grace — attentive. Io listens. Io builds bridges. Io asks you to jump.
